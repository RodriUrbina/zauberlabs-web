Ask around about swapping seats in an E46 and someone will warn you about "the airbags in the seats". It is a reasonable worry, and on many cars of the period it would be right. On the E46 it is wrong in an instructive way: the side airbags are in the **doors**, and a seat swap does not touch them. What a seat swap does touch is less visible and more important: the **seat-belt tensioner** and, on the passenger side, the **occupancy sensor**. Both are part of the airbag system. This article is about that interface, and about what to check before and after you fit a seat.

## The short version

- The BMW parts catalogue lists the E46's side airbags as **front and rear door airbag modules**, under the restraint-system group, on every body style we read: Sedan, Touring, Coupé, Convertible, Compact and M3. No airbag appears in any seat group. [S1, MEDIUM, consistent across six vehicles]
- BMW's own press material confirms the equipment, not the location: **six airbags standard** (driver, front passenger, front side airbags, head airbags) and **automatic seat-belt tensioners for the front seats**. [S2, HIGH for the UK Saloon and Touring, model year 2002]
- The parts that connect a front seat to the airbag system are the **lower belt with its pyrotechnic tensioner**, its **cable to the seat connector**, and the **wiring set for seat-occupancy detection**. The catalogue lists the same wiring items across the body styles. [S1, MEDIUM]
- Owners report that a mismatched or disconnected occupancy sensor leaves the **airbag warning light on** and can **disable the passenger airbag**. [S3, LOW, several concordant reports]
- Mechanically, the seat is a body-group question: Sedan and Touring share seat rails, Coupé and Compact share theirs. [S1, MEDIUM]

## Where the airbags actually are

The catalogue's restraint-system group for the E46 has a sub-group literally called "Side airbag". Its rows are "left front door air bag module", "right front door air bag module", "left rear door airbag module", "right rear door airbag module". That is the pattern on the 330i Sedan and Touring, the 330Ci Coupé and Convertible, the 316ti Compact and the M3 Coupé. The head airbags are listed separately as "ITS head air bag" left and right, and the passenger airbag as a module on its own. [S1, MEDIUM: one mirror, but the same structure on all six vehicles]

The seat groups, by contrast, list frames, rails, controls, head restraints and upholstery by colour. **No airbag module appears in any seat group** we read. [S1, MEDIUM, absence in one mirror]

BMW's press kit for the 2001 facelift puts "six airbags as standard" and lists them as driver and front passenger airbags, front side airbags and ITS head airbags, without saying where the side airbags sit. [S2, HIGH for the list; silent on location] The catalogue supplies the location, the press kit the count. Together they settle the common belief: on an E46, taking a seat out does not remove, disturb or disconnect a side airbag.

## What a seat swap *does* connect to

Under every E46 front seat there is a plug. Behind it, the catalogue shows three things that belong to the airbag system: [S1, MEDIUM]

1. **The lower belt with the belt tensioner.** The buckle side of the front belt is listed as "lower belt with left (or right) belt tensioner", a pyrotechnic unit that pulls the belt tight in a crash. It is fired by the airbag control unit. The catalogue lists three different lower-belt-with-tensioner numbers per side without saying which car gets which. [S1, MEDIUM; the reason for the three variants is NOT ESTABLISHED]
2. **The tensioner cable.** A "cable, seat-belt tensioner / seat connector" and a "cable buckle tensioner" sit in the seat wiring set. The same items appear on Sedan, Coupé, Compact and M3. [S1, MEDIUM]
3. **The occupancy-detection wiring.** A "wiring set, seat occupancy identification" is listed in the seat wiring group of every body style we read. It serves the sensor mat in the passenger cushion that tells the airbag control unit whether the seat is occupied. The catalogue does not say which cars have which sensor generation, and the mat itself is not identified. [S1, MEDIUM; sensor variants NOT ESTABLISHED]

BMW's press kit confirms that automatic belt tensioners were standard on the front seats of the facelift Saloon and Touring. [S2, HIGH] So on any E46 you are likely to be handling at least one pyrotechnic device when you unplug a front seat, and on the passenger side also the sensor that decides whether the passenger airbag arms. That, not a hidden airbag in the backrest, is why a seat swap is safety-sensitive.

## What goes wrong when it goes wrong

The evidence for the consequences is owners' reports, so it is LOW, but the reports agree with each other and with the catalogue's structure: [S3, LOW]

- A **belt tensioner left unplugged**, or plugged into a seat with a different connector, registers as a fault in the airbag control unit and leaves the **airbag warning light on**.
- A **missing or mismatched occupancy sensor** on the passenger side does the same, and can **disable the passenger airbag**, because the control unit cannot confirm the seat is occupied. Bypass emulators and coding changes are sold and discussed for exactly this problem; the catalogue does not list any such part. [S3, LOW; S1 for the absence]
- Swaps **across body groups** (a Coupé seat into a Sedan, for instance) and swaps between seats with **different electrical equipment** (electric adjustment, heating, memory) are the cases owners report most trouble with, because the harness under the seat differs. [S3, LOW]

We found no BMW document describing the consequences, so treat the above as what owners say, not as a BMW statement.

## The mechanical side is the easy side

For the bolt-in part, the catalogue is clearer. Seat rails and mechanisms come in two families: one shared by **Sedan and Touring**, one shared by **Coupé and Compact**. The Convertible has its own frame groups. [S1, MEDIUM] Within a family, the catalogue separates **basic seats** from **BMW sports seats**, and mechanical from electric adjustment, as distinct groups. [S1, MEDIUM]

Owners add detail the catalogue does not: the Sedan's outer rail carries the belt-buckle anchor while the Coupé's belt mounts on the pillar, and Coupé seats recline at a different angle; Sedan seat brackets changed at the 2001 facelift; sport seats drop into a basic car of the same body group with the same bolts. All of that is forum-level and LOW. [S3, LOW] The pattern is consistent, though: within a body group, the mechanical swap is routine, and the work is in the wiring.

Two more things the catalogue makes clear. **Heated-seat installation sets carry the same numbers on Sedan, Touring, Coupé and Compact**, so heating is a body-independent retrofit. [S1, MEDIUM] And **BMW never sold a complete E46 front seat as one part**: the complete-seat pages show only placeholders, and what you can order is frames, rails, controls and covers by colour. What you buy as "a seat" is a used pair, a re-trim or an aftermarket seat. [S1, MEDIUM] That topic has its own article.

## Before you fit a seat

1. **Know your body group.** Sedan and Touring seats interchange mechanically; so do Coupé and Compact. Anything across those lines needs rails and belt-anchor changes and is a job for a specialist. [S1, MEDIUM; S3, LOW]
2. **Compare the plugs before you buy.** The seat you are buying should carry the same connectors under the cushion as the seat you are removing: tensioner lead, and on the passenger side the occupancy wiring. If it does not, you are buying a wiring job, not a seat. [S1, MEDIUM for what the connectors are]
3. **Match the electrical equipment.** Manual to manual, electric to electric, heated to heated, unless you are prepared to add the harness. [S1, MEDIUM for the kits; S3, LOW for the swaps]
4. **Treat the tensioner as live.** It is a pyrotechnic unit. Disconnect the battery before unplugging anything under the seat, observe the airbag-system safety regulations, and run a short test afterwards: these are BMW's own precautions for airbag work. Do not cut, bridge or extend airbag-circuit wiring. [S5, HIGH for the precautions; the wiring rule is general practice, ungraded]
5. **Do not buy a seat that comes with "an airbag emulator".** It means the previous owner had the fault described above and masked it. [S3, LOW]

## After you fit a seat

- **The airbag warning light must go out** after the ignition cycle. If it stays on, the control unit has stored a fault: tensioner, occupancy sensor or both. Have it read out; do not drive on with it lit. [S3, LOW for the typical causes]
- **On the passenger side, have the occupancy detection tested**, not just the light checked. A disabled passenger airbag may not show in a way an owner notices. [S3, LOW]
- **Keep the old seat's tensioner cable and buckle** until the new seat is confirmed working; they are the parts most often needed to make a used seat match the car. [S1, MEDIUM for the parts; S3, LOW for the advice]

## Legal and inspection

Whether a seat swap, or an airbag warning light, affects a roadworthiness inspection depends on your country, and the research behind this article did not cover it. We are not stating anything here until a source is found. [S4, open item]

## Notes on the evidence

**Conflicts recorded.** (1) The common belief that E46 side airbags are in the seats, versus the catalogue's door airbag modules on every body style; the catalogue decides. (2) One web page describing occupancy-sensor connector generations (a four-pin analogue version before 2002, a six-pin digital one after) reads like generated content and contradicts the single occupancy wiring-set number the catalogue lists across bodies; it is recorded as unverified and not used. (3) Owners' "bolt-in" reports versus the wiring and airbag interface: not a contradiction, the electrical side is the caveat.

**Not established (open).** Why the catalogue lists three lower-belt-with-tensioner variants per side and which car gets which; occupancy-sensor generations and whether more than one wiring set exists; Convertible and M3 rail families; the pre- and post-facelift seat-bracket split; which cars had electric, heated, lumbar or memory seats as standard; legal and inspection rules by country. BMW's own parts and workshop systems were not accessible; the catalogue was read through one mirror, which is why its facts are MEDIUM.
